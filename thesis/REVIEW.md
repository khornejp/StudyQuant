# Independent literature-review assessment

The four papers are not a basis for claiming that this project is uniformly stricter or that its direction remains viable. They study materially different targets, horizons, execution mechanisms, and evaluation designs, so none of their headline returns translates into a 45-bar, 0.4%/0.2% triple-barrier result at 4 bps round trip. The polynomial paper is the one exception where a rough cost-only sensitivity can be made: under explicit, strong assumptions its reported Bitcoin return remains positive after 4 bps per inferred round trip, but it still says nothing about the project's 45-bar target. The strongest genuinely relevant contribution is the DRL paper's attempt to measure selection overfitting; however, its CSCV/PBO construction does not validate the project's informal sequential search after the fact. On the project's own stated history, the uncorrected search across roughly 30 configurations and the absence of any researcher-unseen window are the dominant evidential limitations.

## 1. Translation of each headline result to this project's costs and 45-bar horizon

The requested translation is a counterfactual, not a quotation from any paper. A 4 bps round-trip maker-only assumption is not interchangeable with a paper's quoted fee unless the paper supplies the sequence of executed orders, notional, and holding/exit rules. More importantly, none of these papers uses this project's 45-bar triple-barrier label (TP 0.4%, SL 0.2%, forced resolution at bar 45). A forecast/trading return cannot be relabelled as a TP share without its individual paths and orders.

### 1.1 Cohen, *Intraday trading of cryptocurrencies using polynomial auto regression*

The reported headline is Bitcoin NP of $15,580 (15.58% on a stated $100,000 starting portfolio), PF 2.30, and 71.67% profitable trades for the selected 67-minute setup in June--November 2022; buy-and-hold was -44.8% (pp. 9788--9792, especially Table 1 and Section 5). The paper describes a price-crossing system: go long when price is above the fitted polynomial line plus a buffer, short when below, and remain in that position until an opposite signal (p. 9786--9787, Section 3; p. 9787, Figure 2 discussion). It reports no commission, spread, slippage, bid/ask, or other trading cost. Thus its label "net profit" is a portfolio-accounting label, not a return shown net of trading frictions.

**Cost-only estimate, not a 45-bar translation.** The 71.67% Bitcoin win percentage is consistent with **43 wins in 60 completed trades**. Table 2 separately reports 70.00% for longs and 73.33% for shorts, consistent with 21/30 and 22/30, reinforcing an inferred total of 60 position trades (p. 9790, Table 2). If each is one fully invested completed long or short position, 60 round trips at 4 bps cost 2.40 percentage points in simple arithmetic. The reported 15.58% would then be approximately **13.18%**. Applying the cost multiplicatively instead gives \(1.1558(1-0.0004)^{60}-1 \approx 12.82\%\). On those assumptions, the result survives this cost level in sign.

That estimate requires every one of the following assumptions, none fully disclosed by the paper:

- 71.67% is rounded from 43/60, so the denominator really is 60 and all positions are completed;
- each reported "trade" represents one entry and one exit, rather than a fill, signal, or differently counted reversal;
- every position uses the whole $100,000 (or the same full notional), with no leverage, partial allocation, overlapping positions, or cash drag;
- 4 bps applies once per completed position (two maker legs), including a long-to-short reversal treated as closing one position and opening the next;
- the published 15.58% is otherwise gross of all costs and can be adjusted independently of sizing and compounding;
- the unreported execution prices are attainable maker fills at the observed prices, despite the paper using closing-price crossings; and
- no trade is left open beyond the reporting period in a way that changes the count or cost.

Even under that favourable cost-only calculation, **the 45-bar-horizon result cannot be determined**. "67 minutes" is the selected bar/model configuration, not a 45-bar triple-barrier holding rule; the paper supplies neither TP/SL barriers nor the per-trade holding durations/outcomes needed to compute a 45-bar TP share. Its Table 1 does include a *45-minute configuration* with Bitcoin NP $876.80, PF 1.05 and PP 55.42%, but that is a model-bar setting, not a 45-bar horizon, and it still has no cost disclosure (p. 9789, Table 1). Those figures therefore do not survive as project-comparable results.

### 1.2 Zbikowski, *Application of Machine Learning Algorithms for Bitcoin Automated Trading*

The headline trading result is a 33.52% table return for volume-weighted SVM (VW-SVM), versus 10.58% for Box SVM and 4.86% buy-and-hold, on 15-minute Bitstamp aggregates from 9 January to 2 February 2015 (pp. 5--6, Sections 3--4 and Table 2). The prose says 33.58%, an internal 0.06-point inconsistency; Table 2 is the appropriate precise reported value. The paper assumes a 0.2% transaction fee (p. 5, Section 3), allows both long and short even though that was not possible through the stated Bitstamp account, and commits all current capital whenever a position is open (p. 5, Section 4).

**Cannot be determined under 4 bps and a 45-bar horizon.** The 33.52% (or prose 33.58%) is after the paper's stated 0.2% fee assumption, but it does not report order count, whether 0.2% is per side or round trip, per-order notional history, fills, spread/slippage, or the realised holding periods. Its 15-minute aggregates and walk-forward re-optimization every 100 quotations from the previous 200 are disclosed (p. 2, Section 2.1), but no 45-bar barrier rule or TP-share statistic is. Consequently neither its return nor drawdown can be recomputed under 4 bps, and none is evidence that it clears a 40% TP share at this project's horizon.

### 1.3 Lua et al., *Automated Bitcoin Trading dApp Using Price Prediction from a Deep Learning Model*

The headline is LSTM ROI of 488.74% ($4,887.47 profit on $1,000), versus 234.68% buy-and-hold, over 9 December 2022--23 May 2024 (pp. 19, 21--22, Table 7 and Section 5). It is a daily close-price portfolio switcher between wBTC and USDC on Uniswap, driven by a predicted closing price and optionally MACD filters, not a fixed-horizon long/short barrier trade (pp. 8--10, Sections 3.1--3.2 and Table 1). The authors model a **0.5% commission per transaction**; Table 7 reports 45 LSTM trades (pp. 9--10, Table 1; p. 19, Table 7). The tested dataset is chronologically split 70%/15%/15%, with the final test period stated above (pp. 15--16, Section 3.5).

**Cannot be determined under the project model.** Although both a fee and a trade count are stated, the paper does not disclose the executed daily order sequence, each swap notional after the all-in wBTC/USDC changes, pool price impact, gas, spread/MEV, or enough fill data to recompute the equity curve at 4 bps. A Uniswap swap is also not an assumed maker/maker limit-order fill. Reducing a modeled 50 bps per transaction to 4 bps would mechanically make the reported backtest look better if all else were held fixed, but the exact revised ROI cannot be derived from aggregate Table 7. Forty-five trades is a count over roughly 17.5 months, **not** a 45-bar holding horizon; the paper reports win ratio, not this project's 45-bar TP-before-SL share (p. 19, Table 7). Its 488.74% therefore does not translate.

### 1.4 Anonymous ICLR submission, *Deep Reinforcement Learning for Cryptocurrency Trading: Practical Approach to Address Backtest Overfitting*

Its selected PPO agent reports -34.96% cumulative return over 1 May--27 June 2022, versus -47.78% equal weight and -50.78% S&P BDM index, with PBO estimate 8.0%; the paper's other agents and conventional approaches are also negative (p. 9, Table 3). It uses five-minute data, 10 assets, no shorting, 0.3% of trade value per trade, market-price execution, and explicitly ignores slippage because high volume is taken to imply liquidity (pp. 4, 7, Sections 3.2.1 and 5.1). Its agent can buy/sell/hold portfolio quantities and can liquidate under a CVIX rule, not follow TP/SL barriers (pp. 3--5, Sections 3.1--3.3).

**Cannot be determined under 4 bps and a 45-bar horizon.** The 0.3% cost is disclosed, but aggregate return alone cannot be repriced without the number, timing and notional of rebalances. There is no 45-bar barrier target or TP share to transform. What *does* survive translation is only the paper's methodological result: its PBO is a rank-based validation diagnostic for a specified ensemble of trials, not an economic return, and it remains conceptually distinct from the project's cost model (pp. 5--7, Sections 4.1--4.2).

## 2. Methods not already shown to be exhausted, and whether they change the conclusion

The papers contain several model families not stated in the project position as already tried: PAR crossing signals; EMA/Box-SVM/VW-SVM with technical indicators; daily RF/LSTM/Bi-LSTM with 30 exogenous/on-chain/sentiment features; and multi-asset DRL/CVIX control. That fact alone does not make any of them an answer to either closed-book question. Their published targets are directional price/portfolio return, not the 45-bar TP-before-SL event. Treating them as promising because their reported ROI is large would repeat the non-comparable-headline error.

| Candidate method from the papers | Direct relevance to the two problems | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| PAR, Box-SVM/VW-SVM, RF, LSTM or Bi-LSTM on the same project data and labels | These are alternative predictors. They could be evaluated for TP share, but no paper gives a reason they should add the required 6--7 percentage points under this label. | **(i) One more configuration.** It adds to, rather than corrects, the approximately 30-result selection burden. |
| EMA/MACD filters, threshold/buffer selection, or CVIX risk gate | These gate or reshape entries/exposure. They do not supply an independently validated 45-bar edge. Several are plainly further strategy configurations. | **(i) One more configuration.** |
| dApp paper's 30-feature data set (other crypto opens, market indexes, commodities, on-chain measures, fear/greed) | This is the one potential **different data source/edge mechanism** rather than merely a different learner. It could address TP share only after point-in-time availability, alignment and leakage are audited. The paper forward-fills unavailable/new-asset values and uses daily data, so it is not ready-made for a 45-bar intraday test (pp. 15--16, Section 3.5 and Table 3). | **(ii), conditionally.** Acquiring and auditing genuinely new, timestamped information and evaluating it on future or otherwise researcher-unseen data could change what is concluded. Re-running it as features on spent windows is still (i). |
| DRL paper's combinatorially symmetric CV/PBO process | It addresses validation after a *predefined* search, not TP share. It could change the conclusion only if used prospectively with a fixed candidate set and suitable new/resequestered data. | **(ii), prospectively only.** It cannot retrospectively certify the existing sequential search; see Section 3. |
| The dApp's smart-contract/oracle deployment | It improves custody/auditability of a deployment architecture, not predictive edge or post-selection statistical validity (pp. 8--10, Sections 3.1--3.2). | **Neither a solution to (a) nor (b).** |

The conservative answer is therefore: no already-published model in these four directly solves (a) or rescues (b). The only routes that could change the conclusion are a truly new information set/edge mechanism, or a prospective evaluation design fixed before new observations are seen. Neither is a license to mine the spent data again.

## 3. The DRL paper's probability-of-backtest-overfitting approach

### 3.1 Applicability to the project's sequential search

Not as a valid PBO estimate. The paper constructs a matrix whose columns are **H hyperparameter trials**, each trained/evaluated on the same predefined combinatorial splits; it then repeatedly separates the rows into in-sample and out-of-sample portions and asks whether the configuration selected in-sample ranks below the OOS median (pp. 6--7, Section 4.2, equations 8--12). Its experiment chooses 50 trial settings from a described 2,700-combination hyperparameter space, with N=5, k=2 and 10 combinatorial splits (pp. 7--8, Sections 5.1--5.2 and Table 2).

That construction needs configurations whose performance rows can reasonably be treated symmetrically across the splits and whose search set is defined independently of their observed test payoffs. The project's process was instead informal and sequential: inspect a fold-test payoff, choose the next gate/threshold/direction/barrier/model configuration conditional on it, then inspect again. Later candidates are therefore functions of earlier outcomes; they are neither an exchangeable predefined grid nor independent trials. Financial time series also make the paper's simplified split assumptions demanding in the first place; the paper itself motivates CSCV by criticising ordinary iid k-fold CV (p. 2, Section 2.2). Applying its output as a probability to the existing history would attach a precise-looking number to an invalid reference set.

### 3.2 Could it be estimated from existing artifacts, or would it require reruns?

There are two different answers.

- A **mechanical post-hoc diagnostic** may be computable without rerunning *if* the repository retains, for every attempted configuration, aligned per-split/per-time performance (not merely a final fold mean), the exact split boundaries, all failed and discarded attempts, and a predeclared performance metric. The project documents fold-test payoff artifacts and an attempt ledger, so a rank-resampling exercise may be possible from those artifacts.
- A **valid PBO inference for the actual search** cannot be recovered merely by computation. Its prerequisites were absent when choices were made: a fixed candidate universe, comparable CSCV return matrix generated for all candidates, and selection not conditioned on the same fold tests. Reconstructing a cleaned matrix after seeing results does not make the configurations exchangeable. To use the paper's method honestly for a new claim would require a prospective, frozen candidate set and re-evaluation on data that have not been used by the researcher for selection. Given that all clean windows are spent, that means new future data (or a genuinely separate, previously unread data source with an unspent evaluation segment), not another run on the old windows.

### 3.3 What it would likely say about the observed best t approximately 2.47

It would not honestly yield a defensible numerical PBO for this history, for the mismatch above. Qualitatively, however, it should increase rather than reduce concern: about 30 attempts were viewed against fold-test payoffs, no multiplicity correction was applied, and the best reported t is about 2.47. Under a rough independent standard-normal null, a one-sided t at least 2.47 has probability about 0.0068; across 30 tries the chance of at least one such result is about \(1-(1-0.0068)^{30}\approx18.5\%\). This is only an intuition check—not a valid correction, because attempts and folds are dependent—but it shows why t=2.47 is weak evidence after this search. A CSCV-like ranking diagnostic built from the history would likely show substantial instability/selection risk, not establish that the best configuration is real. The appropriate conclusion remains **inconclusive, not validated and not quantified by a valid post-hoc PBO**.

### Papers stronger than this project on X

- **Multiple-comparison control:** the DRL paper, through its predefined CSCV/PBO procedure (pp. 5--9).
- **Publication-time held-out-test discipline:** the DRL and dApp papers have stated final chronological test intervals not documented as already spent by an informal sequential search (DRL p. 7; Lua et al. pp. 16--18).
- **Disclosed trade count/sample for the reported best result:** Cohen, with an inferred 60 Bitcoin position trades versus the project's preregistered 50 trades; this is a narrow sample-count comparison, not proof of better inference (Cohen p. 9790, Table 2).
- **Concrete deployment architecture:** the dApp paper, for specifying a smart-contract/oracle/Uniswap system; this is stronger on system architecture, not on the validity of its backtest return (Lua et al. pp. 8--10).

The resulting verdict is deliberately non-triumphal: the project is more candid and in some areas more explicit, but it is currently weaker on post-selection inference and cannot turn its spent data into an edge claim. The literature offers possible future model and data directions, not a statistical exemption from that constraint.
